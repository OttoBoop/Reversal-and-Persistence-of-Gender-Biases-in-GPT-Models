## Your paper in one paragraph

Your paper is an **empirical, econometric audit** of gender bias across 14+ GPT models
(62k+ generated stories; three prompt-based experiments), estimated with an **OLS
linear-probability model** (robust SEs; coefficients read as percentage points, e.g.
β₁ = −0.61 → −61 pp on P(male character)). Its headline result is **"overcorrection"** —
aligned chat models (GPT-3.5+) swing to a pro-female association (β ≈ −0.61) while older
completion GPT-3 models were near-neutral (β ≈ −0.03), and occupational stereotypes persist
(all receptionists female, 98% blue-collar male). It is currently styled for **Public Choice**
(Springer) on the Motoki et al. (2024) template: author-date citations (natbib/apalike), a
~229-word **two-paragraph abstract that embeds 8 citations**, ~5,500 words of main prose +
10 figures / 6 tables + a large appendix (20+ regressions), with **no Discussion section,
no formal hypotheses, and no theory-building section**. It is reproducibility-heavy (Jupyter
notebook, CSVs, 896 numeric audit checks) and carries 5 keywords.

## AI & Society (Springer)
**One-sentence case.** AI & SOCIETY actively and currently publishes exactly your topic — empirical LLM/GPT gender-bias audits — and treats them as serious sociotechnical contributions, so your large multi-model "overcorrection" study has a real, demonstrated home here provided you reframe its register from econometric report to society-facing argument.

**Why it fits your paper (the affirmative case).** The journal's scope explicitly covers the "cultural, social, cognitive, economic, ethical, and philosophical implications" of AI, and the evidence that GPT/LLM gender-bias work belongs here is not speculative: there is a visible 2023–2026 cluster of papers in this exact lane (an NLP audit of ChatGPT gender bias in hiring, a critical discourse analysis of GPT-3's gender/race biases, and a justice-oriented algorithmic-fairness framework). Your paper sits naturally in that conversation, and your "economic implications" angle is named in scope as well, so you are not smuggling an economics paper into an alien venue.

What the journal rewards is methodological soundness *and empirical strength* wrapped in social-theoretical and ethical framing — research articles must be "strictly focused, academically coherent, theoretically grounded, methodologically sound and empirically strong." Your study is unusually strong on the empirical axis: 14+ models, 62k+ generated stories, three prompt-based experiments, and a reproducibility apparatus (Jupyter notebook, CSVs, 896 numeric audit checks) that exceeds what most AS empirical papers carry. That rigor is an asset here, not a liability — the journal publishes computational/NLP audits, and your scale and reproducibility distinguish you from the smaller-sample qualitative pieces that dominate the cluster.

The "overcorrection" finding is the natural hook for the framing AS wants. The journal's most-cited bias papers lead by problematizing the "alignment / AI removes bias" narrative; your headline result — that aligned chat models swing to a pro-female association (beta about −0.61) while older completion models were near-neutral (beta about −0.03), yet occupational stereotypes persist (all receptionists female, 98% blue-collar male) — is precisely a critique of the "newer models are fairer" story, backed by a coefficient. **The honest counter-case.** The real mismatch is register, not topic. Your paper is written in an econometrician's voice — OLS linear-probability models, robust SEs, coefficients-as-percentage-points, 20+ appendix regressions, no Discussion, no theory section — and AS readers (philosophers of technology, STS, HCI, critical social theorists) will want the same numbers reframed as evidence about a sociotechnical harm, with gendered-language and algorithmic-fairness *theory* foregrounded and the ethical/policy stakes made explicit. A pure leaderboard-and-tables presentation would read as out of place even though it would be admissible. Concretely, you would need to add a genuine theory/framing section, add a Discussion + Implications section, translate every coefficient for a non-economist reader, move the densest regressions to anonymized supplementary material, and ideally co-bill a named contribution (e.g. your prompt-based audit pipeline / 896-check protocol as a reusable framework, in the manner of the Sivakaminathan & Musi paper).

Secondarily, this is a hybrid journal with a substantial open-access APC, and double-blind anonymization is an easily-missed hard gate given your named, reproducible artifacts. If your goal is to reach an economics/public-choice audience or to keep the paper in its current econometric idiom with minimal rewriting, this is not the lowest-effort home.

**Bottom line.** Choose AI & SOCIETY if you want your gender-bias audit read as a sociotechnical and ethical contribution by an interdisciplinary AI-and-society audience, and you are willing to reframe it accordingly — the topic fit and the empirical/reproducibility strengths are genuinely on your side. The single biggest thing it would require is rhetorical, not mechanical: adding a theory/framing section and a Discussion/Implications section, and re-presenting the "overcorrection" result as evidence in a values-first argument rather than as a regression output.

## Journal of Business Ethics (Springer)

**One-sentence case.** Choose JBE if you want your audit to land in the single most prominent, highly-cited business-ethics venue and you are willing to reframe the "overcorrection" finding as a moral and managerial problem — because JBE has now demonstrably published a direct LLM gender-bias audit, so your method is no longer foreign to the journal.

**Why it fits your paper (the affirmative case).** The topic is squarely in scope. JBE's aims-and-scope reads "business" and "ethics" expansively and explicitly welcomes "reports of empirical research" in "any quantitative or qualitative methodological tradition" ([aims and scope](https://link.springer.com/journal/10551/aims-and-scope)). Algorithmic gender bias — discrimination, fairness, and the ethics of AI systems that feed hiring, HR, and marketing — is exactly the kind of phenomenon JBE analyzes "from a moral viewpoint." Your paper's subject matter is not a stretch; it is on-mission.

What changed the fit picture materially is precedent. The journal has shown it will publish exactly this kind of computational audit when it is framed as an ethics contribution.

JBE rewards three things your paper can supply. ## Decision summary

Ranked for **this specific empirical GPT-gender-bias paper** (1 = best fit), weighing topical-scope fit against stylistic adaptation cost:

1. **AI & Society — best fit.** *Strongest reason:* it actively publishes empirical LLM/GPT gender-bias audits as serious contributions ([Sivakaminathan & Musi 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02564-8); [Palacios Barea et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-023-01804-z)), and your author-date citations, caption conventions, and keyword count already align. *Biggest cost:* a register shift — add a theory/framing section and a Discussion + Implications section and translate the econometrics for non-economists (**MEDIUM**; no new data).

2. **Journal of Business Ethics.** *Strongest reason:* a direct LLM gender-bias audit now has precedent here ([Cao et al. 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-025-06216-1)), the venue explicitly welcomes quantitative empirical work, and your reproducibility package exceeds its data-availability bar. *Biggest cost:* it demands an explicit *ethics* contribution — a theory-development section plus a full Discussion/Implications (theoretical + managerial), turning the regression into evidence for a moral argument (**HIGH**; ~a third of new framing prose).

3. **PACMHCI.** *Strongest reason:* the CSCW community prizes AI auditing ([WeAudit, CSCW 2025 Best Paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3757702)) and imposes no length cap, so your large reproducible study is welcome. *Biggest cost:* a re-genre-ing — switch to `acmart` and numeric `[n]` citations (reversing your author-date direction), add CSCW-theory grounding and a Discussion/Implications section, and ideally add a qualitative layer; stand-alone audits more often fit FAccT/AIES/EAAMO (**HIGH**).

4. **JAIR.** *Strongest reason:* free CC BY open access, a standing [Fairness & Bias in AI Special Track](https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/SpecialTrack-FBAI), and an online-appendix norm your reproducibility apparatus fits perfectly. *Biggest cost:* the product-audit genre has essentially no precedent here; you would have to reposition the work as a *measurement/methodology* contribution and substantially expand it ([van der Wal et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.15195) shows the expected register) (**HIGH**).

**Recommendation.** If the goal is the *lowest-effort home that still broadens your audience beyond economics*, target **AI & Society** — the topic fit is proven and the work is a reframing rather than a rewrite. If the goal is *reach into the business/management-ethics community*, **Journal of Business Ethics** is now a credible second (the Cao et al. precedent removes the old "wrong method" objection), at the cost of a genuine ethics-and-theory rebuild. **PACMHCI** and **JAIR** are viable only if you are prepared to re-genre the paper; otherwise your current **Public Choice** target remains the most natural home for the econometric-audit form as-is.

**Next step.** Pick a target, and the manuscript can then be adapted to it — starting with the cross-cutting edits every one of these venues wants (collapse the abstract to one citation-free paragraph; add a theory/framing section and a Discussion/Implications section) before the venue-specific reformatting.
